For thirty years, Elias tended the Thornlight, sweeping its beam across the black water every dusk. Sailors credited the light with saving their lives. Elias credited nothing but coffee and aching knees.

Then the boats stopped coming.

First it was a single hull, gone missing in a fog that smelled wrong—sweet, like forgotten birthdays. Then two, then a whole convoy whose last radio burst dissolved into static shaped like whispering. The coast guard wrote it off as bad luck. Elias wrote it off as nothing, because a keeper does not question the sea.

But one night, during the new moon, he saw them.

They rose from the water where his beam touched—tall, translucent figures, drifting upward like smoke in reverse. Not drowning. Ascending. They followed the light, reaching toward the lamp as flowers reach for sun, their faces slack with a peace so absolute it made his chest tighten. One of them, small and shimmering, pressed a wet hand against the glass and pointed past him, toward the mechanism that turned the great eye.

Elias understood then, in the way you understand a dream you've always known. The ships had never been the point. The light was a door, and every night he opened it, and the dead walked through, guided home to whatever waited beyond the salt and the dark. His mother had drowned the year he turned seven. She had been in the front row.

He had kept the lamp burning for thirty years, and every evening he had walked her out of the sea.

That night Elias did not extinguish the light at dawn. He let it burn through the daylight, a foolish thing to do, wasting oil, breaking every rule. He simply sat by the glass and waited, because he had work to do, and questions, and thirty years of things left unsaid.
