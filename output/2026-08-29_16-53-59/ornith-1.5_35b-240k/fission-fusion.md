**Answer (as scored):**

**1. Basic physical process**

Nuclear fission splits heavy atomic nuclei (e.g., uranium-235 or plutonium-239) into lighter fragments. A neutron strikes a heavy nucleus, making it unstable; it splits, releasing energy plus additional neutrons that can trigger a self-sustaining chain reaction.

Nuclear fusion combines light nuclei (typically the hydrogen isotopes deuterium and tritium) into a heavier nucleus (helium). Because nuclei are positively charged, they must be forced together against their mutual electrostatic repulsion.

**2. Energy requirements and output**

Fission is easy to initiate—thermal (slow) neutrons can cause it—and each event releases about 200 MeV. Fusion requires enormous input energy to reach ignition temperatures (over 100 million degrees Celsius) to overcome Coulomb repulsion. However, fusion releases more energy per unit mass of fuel—roughly three to four times that of fission—with the D-T reaction yielding about 17.6 MeV per event.

**3. Current real-world applications**

Fission is commercially mature, supplying about 10% of global electricity, powering nuclear submarines and aircraft carriers, and used in weapons and medical/research isotopes. Fusion has no commercial power use yet; it is confined to experimental reactors (e.g., ITER, JET) and hydrogen bombs, and it powers the Sun and stars.

**4. Engineering challenges preventing widespread fusion**

The central problem is achieving net energy gain (producing more energy than consumed). Key challenges include confining a plasma at extreme temperatures via magnetic (tokamak/stellarator) or inertial methods, developing materials that survive intense neutron bombardment and heat flux, securing a tritium fuel supply (requiring on-site breeding from lithium), sustaining continuous operation, and making the technology economically viable.

**5. Safety considerations**

Fission carries risks of core meltdown, long-lived radioactive waste needing secure storage for millennia, and nuclear-proliferation concerns. Fusion produces no meltdown risk and no long-lived high-level waste (reactor components become radioactive but for far shorter periods, ~a century), cannot undergo a runaway chain reaction, and has lower proliferation risk—though it emits neutron radiation and tritium is itself radioactive.
